Following training, the researchers compared the predictions of two versions of the model: one
trained on a dataset containing the AI-generated descriptions and the other trained on a dataset
containing text from papers only. They found that the model trained on AI-generated descriptions
successfully learned how to align geometric structures with text descriptions while the other did
not.
“We wanted to create an AI model that works broadly across materials,” Li says. “That’s why we
decided to synthesize narratives rather than rely solely on existing papers. We found when we used
that narrative dataset, the models are better at predicting the function.”
Accelerating materials science
The researchers then showed their model could be given a natural language query describing a
property and identify crystalline materials with that property, outperforming a baseline.
“There are sometimes these step changes within materials science around algorithmic integration
toward useful tools, which the community uses to further advance the field,” Olivetti says. “This
approach helps us see more deeply into three-dimensional structures and link structures to property
and processing. I hope this can be one of those step changes.”
Li compares the model to AlphaFold, an AI model that links protein structures with function that has
helped advance biological research and drug development.
The researchers believe their new approach can work with many crystalline materials, and they hope
other material scientists will use it to accelerate their work.
“Being able to see a three-dimensional crystal and how its atoms are connected is super
foundational,” Li says. “I think this will be useful to anyone who practices materials science.”
The work was supported, in part, by the U.S. Defense Threat Reduction Agency through the University
Research Alliance; the U.S. Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency; and the National Research
Foundation of Korea.
