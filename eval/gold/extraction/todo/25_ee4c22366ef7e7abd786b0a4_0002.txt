However, since we started with a default container, many legacy critical use cases were
unfortunately stuck there without a data migration. To protect critical services that still relied
on data in the shared default container, we often advised teams to create separate containers for
new services or major product features. As we scaled, pressure on the default container grew and
production incidents also grew. We began to provision containers for any new service or large
product feature just to protect the default container from more load.
This saved us from many site-wide outages, limiting the blast radius to feature or service
degradation outages. However, the overhead of provisioning new containers eventually caught up to
us:
Lack of stable aggregate growth
In the old shared default container, any particular new use case going from 0 QPS to 1 million QPS
was unlikely to significantly shift the total aggregate usage. On an isolated container however, a
new use case going from 0 to 1 million QPS might represent 1,000x more load than the baseline
attributed to only internal dogfooding.
With dedicated containers, teams now needed to remember to pre-provision containers to the expected
level of throughput for their launches. Product teams would often ship their features to production
and forget to pre-scale and we’d sit in SEV rooms on launch day attempting to scale up containers
last minute.
This is a core workload mixing benefit of multi-tenant systems that we lose in favor of better
isolation with this container strategy.
Capacity planning
Over time, monitoring usage of dedicated containers also required intimate product knowledge of when
traffic would shift or grow.
We can provide a basic control plane script to scale up the provisioned throughput for each
container based on growth trends, but without the stable aggregate growth, scaling these correctly
required essentially every product team at OpenAI to be involved in capacity planning.
This failed to scale organizationally with a wide variance in how much diligence each service’s
owning team paid to their Azure Cosmos DB provisioning.
Phase III: Using multi-tenancy by default, with data movement capability
As the organization grew, those operational problems became difficult to scale. No lone, central
team doing planning resulted in many teams planning poorly. As a result, we ended up shifting back
towards a multi-tenant solution and worked closely with the Azure team to engineer fixes for our
original multi-tenancy issues with a shared-tenancy design:
Hot key throttling
Throttling the hottest keys first prevents noisy neighbors from consuming all of the available
throughput for a given physical partition. This reduces the noisy neighbor effect, where one hot key
can have a site-wide blast radius.
Quality of Service (QoS)
Request tiering QoS similarly prevents offline workloads, such as bulk inserts or data extracts from
utilizing all of the throughput for a given physical partition. By assigning the requests for these
workloads a lower priority, they are throttled first, preventing throttling on higher priority,
interactive requests that serve end users.
Serverless mode
Azure Cosmos DB already had a serverless mode that would’ve fixed our weak partitions problem with
the provisioned mode. However, there was a major downside: it scaled based on storage usage only and
limited RU usage to 5K RU/s per physical partition. This would solve our weak partitions problem,
but prevent us from scaling up RU-heavy use cases.
A new improved serverless mode Azure Cosmos DB team built for us allowed every physical partition to
scale up to 10K RU/s and introduced RU-usage-based splits in addition to storage-usage-based splits
allowed us to stop monitoring and scaling up containers manually.
