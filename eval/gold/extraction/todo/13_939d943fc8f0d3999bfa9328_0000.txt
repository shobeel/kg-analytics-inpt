The always-on AI personal assistant, which keeps working in the background even when you aren’t
asking it to, is this year’s answer to the question of how people will actually use deep learning
models. The startup Instinct became an overnight sensation, and Meta put the full force of its
marketing behind its assistant, Muse.
OpenAI wasn’t going to be left out. The company released dots, its assistant offering, at its
developer day in late September. Alexander Embiricos, who leads OpenAI’s enterprise product team,
joined us at TechCrunch Disrupt this month to explain where the product came from — starting with
the question of why so many companies seem to be turning to cutesy mascots as avatars for their
trillion-parameter matrix multiplication machines.
“There’s kind of this convergent evolution happening,” Embiricos said of the efforts to build AI
assistants. “As we were working on the product, we realized if we could frame this as, it’s an
entity and it’s a single conversation, not many different conversations....it starts to become
intuitive.”
The assistant is available in the ChatGPT desktop and mobile apps to subscribers on the Pro tier or
higher, with plans starting at $100 a month. Once it is connected to your calendar, email, computer
and other tools, the agent tries to help you get your work done.
Embiricos points out that you could achieve much of dots’ functionality with Open AI’s Codex app,
its coding tool, released at the beginning of this year. But that requires more work and hand-
holding. He recalled celebrating when his wife started leaving her computer on all night so that her
agents could keep working.
“On the product team, what I think about is, how do we make this intuitive and useful to people?” he
told Disrupt attendees. “A lot of that isn’t building some new piece of tech into the product; it’s
how you frame it to the user and how it feels.”
The path from chatbot to coding tool to personal assistant
Making the technology feel intuitive meant repackaging the functionality of the company’s models
into something more accessible, a path it was already on as it spun ChatGPT Work out of the agentic
coding tools it had built for software engineers. Even the name — a play on the negative space
inside of OpenAI’s logo — is intended “to feel like just something that could eventually be
ubiquitous.” (Asked when he learned that rival AI entrepreneur Elon Musk owns the dot.com URL,
Embiricos said, “I found out very late.”)
The idea of a persistent agent is that it doesn’t need prompting. For OpenAI, that means more token
usage, and for users, a more pro-active assistant “that’s sort of thinking about you and thinking
how to help you,” as Embiricos put it.
In our testing, the assistant’s ability to think ahead is apparent and often useful, but it isn’t
quite there yet. It will flag calendar conflicts and upcoming deadlines, but not necessarily in the
most timely fashion.
“The first time it told me about one of those [conflicts] — this is like early in development — it
told me three minutes before the thing,” Embiricos says of his dot. “It’s like, ‘Hey, this thing is
coming up. You might want to do something about it.’ I only have three minutes!”
Embiricos expects the timing to improve over time, but the proactive approach is key to the product.
“I was hearing about an engineer at a drone delivery startup, and they were saying, ‘We had this
outage, and by the time I noticed that the outage was going on, my dot had already noticed the
outage was happening, had already analyzed the outage, and had come to me with useful information
for how we could resolve the outage.’”
Giving dot the keys
As with most AI agent use cases, there’s a tension between the personal information an AI model
needs to be useful and exactly how much personal data you are exposing to a software company. Muse
has been criticized for the profiles it builds of your family and friends, which the software uses
to know, say, what kind of restaurant you might want to go to with a specific pal, but could also
add incredible value to Meta’s advertising business. Does dots collect similar data?
“Yes, we have a thing that we built into dots, which we call proactive research,” Embiricos said.
“On a cadence, it will think about what it knows about you, what is the conversation that it’s had,
and then what have you given it access to that it can think about, and then it will go and research,
‘What could I do to be more helpful to you?’ And then, basically, it will propose that.”
It’s difficult to separate the data needs of the agent from that of its parent corporation. Hark, a
startup developing its own AI personal assistant, is explicit in pointing out that it doesn’t
collect data to fuel an advertising business the way that OpenAI and Meta do.
